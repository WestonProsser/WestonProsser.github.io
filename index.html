Sierra College circuits lab · 2025

8-Bit Light Meter

A light meter made purely from logic chips. No microcontroller and no code. It turns light into an 8-bit number, with the ability to subtract the room's current light level, and shows the remaining output on the LEDs.

It started as a lab in my circuits class at Sierra College. The experience of working as a team, designing, testing, and debugging became the foundation for my future work.

We were only given the outline of the circuit. The rest was for us to figure out. It was built in a group of four, where I took the lead design role. Afterwards, I turned this design into my first PCB, designed in EasyEDA.

01

Reading the light

A photoresistor sits inside a black PLA housing, attached to a Wheatstone bridge. As the light level changes, the bridge output swings between roughly 0 V and 5 V.

One of the major challenges with this circuit was finding a resistor combination that would give a close-to-linear output. A photoresistor in a Wheatstone bridge naturally gives a non-linear result when the resistor values are not tuned properly. Getting 2.5 V at ambient light, with even swings up to 5 V and down to 0 V, took hours of testing resistor combinations. The output never quite reached 0 V, so the goal was the combination that got as close to both ends as possible.

Figure 2 documents this process: repeated testing until the desired results were achieved.

The ADC0804 takes the voltage and turns it into a binary number from 0 to 255.

02

Zeroing out the room

Once roughly 2.5 V was achieved at ambient light levels, we built this into our circuit. However, to make sure the circuit worked in any condition, a "tare" feature was added.

Eight switches sit in the bottom left (Figure 3), allowing the user to zero the reading at any desired light level.

This works using XOR gates, controlled by the SPDT switch seen in the bottom right (Figure 3), and two 4-bit adders. Together, the XOR gates and adders take the switch number off the reading, so the display reads zero in the chosen room light.

This is done through two's complement subtraction, which I learned in order to build this circuit.

03

Hours of debugging

This was easily one of the longest projects I've ever worked on. Countless hours went into debugging my own circuit and other lab groups' circuits. Some of the problems encountered were:

  • Bridge resistors. Getting the bridge output as close to 0 V and 5 V as possible.
  • LED resistors. Bright enough to read, dim enough to not outshine the other LEDs.
  • Reset button. The converter needed a push-button reset. I ended up pulling pins off the button and reworking the wiring so it only touches ground while it's pressed, and doesn't accidentally bridge a connection while unpressed.
  • Zeroing. Built from the switches and XOR gates, once I understood how binary subtraction works.
  • Moving to chips. Scaling up from transistor circuits to logic chips, and debugging each chip as it went in. This ate up far too many hours, as each chip had a unique quirk and it was my first time using ICs.
  • Layout. Arranging the LEDs and wires so I could trace any signal by eye. It was a pain, but it paid off every time something broke.

Each IC added more complexity and took a number of hours to implement. While each IC saved space, supplying a clean voltage, working out which pins should do what, and understanding the internal logic became a challenge.

While this was once a nightmare, it is now a regular part of my design process, and it taught me a lot about design revisions and debugging.

04

From breadboard to PCB

Once the class ended, I created a PCB version. This was out of my own interest, to see if I could create a PCB.

The board came back and all the components fit. However, because I transferred to UCSD, the board was never soldered and tested.

Yonder Dynamics · 2026 · Version 2

Limit Switch Endstop

A PCB designed to detect when the rover's linear slide has reached the end of its rail. It works in tandem with an ODrive S1 or Pro, using a single limit switch and one transistor. Its signal is only valid while the board has power and the wiring is unbroken.

  • Hand soldered

Lever switch on its normally-closed contact, or a remote switch on J2
Board
2 layers, ground on both
Cost
About $10 a board, in a batch of five

Bench tested. Testing on the slide is next.
01

What it has to do

The rover's arm has multiple axes of freedom. The arm itself runs on a linear slide, driven by a NEO 550 motor through an ODrive. The rail needs some form of end detection so the motor can return to its middle position.

Version 1, a Hall-effect sensor, worked, but reliability concerns were raised over the placement of the magnet.

This led to an internal discussion that ended in a switch from the Hall-effect sensor to a limit switch. The primary reason was that if the magnet ever fell off, the endstop would fail. The software team also liked the idea of a physical switch.

The downsides are that the switch is slower and less precise than the Hall sensor, but for this application it all stays well within tolerance.

02

The circuit

The switch is wired through its normally-closed contact. At rest the loop is closed, which turns on a PNP transistor, and the transistor supplies about 4.9 V to the ODrive's input. The status LED turns on, indicating an "OK" state.

Once the switch is pressed, the loop opens and the transistor turns off. A pull-down resistor drags the signal output down to 0 V and the LED turns off. The ODrive reads this as an "end of slide" state.

If a cable were cut, a connector came unplugged, or the 5 V supply dropped out, the ODrive would see the same thing as the switch being pressed. The board has to stay powered and be wired correctly for an "OK" state. Each of these faults reads as a stop.

This is why the PNP transistor is on the high side of the board. The ODrive also has no internal pull-down resistor of its own, which is why there is one on the signal output.

The remaining small capacitors filter the switch and signal lines, aiding the ODrive's own 50 ms of debouncing.

03

The layout

The layout is two layers, with a ground pour on both. All components sit on the top side, as this was the easiest choice for hand soldering.

The second JST connector, J2, is a way to move the limit switch to any location desired: take it off the board and connect it back through the 2-pin JST connector.

There are three test points (switch, ground, and signal), each big enough to clip a probe onto.

04

Build and bench test

The board was ordered from JLCPCB and the parts were sourced from Digi-Key. All hand soldered by me.

In bench testing, all outputs read as they should.

05

What's next

Testing on the slide is all that remains. Once the mechanical team has made a mount, I will configure the ODrive, test the endstop, and record my results.

The initial configuration for the ODrive is GPIO 7 as a digital input, set as the minimum endstop, active-high off, 50 ms debounce, and a slow homing speed to start.

Hall-Effect Endstop

Version 1 of the rover's end-of-travel sensor. It went from a notebook sketch to a breadboard to a hand-wired board on the slide.

01

The job

During the 2025–2026 design year, a new linear rail was added to give the arm another axis of freedom. For the operators to know the position of the arm, a homing sequence needed to be created. There was also no detection to indicate when the slide had reached the end of the rail.

The ODrive could handle many of these features, but a physical sensor and a software setup were in order.

02

On paper first

I worked out the requirements and the general idea on paper: the ODrive's GPIO, the slide's mechanical constraints, and the circuit diagram to be implemented.

Notably, version 2 took heavy inspiration from version 1. It carries over the normally-closed (NC) wiring noted on this page, for the reason written there.

03

Breadboard test

The first tests were performed on a breadboard, using an ESP32 to read the Hall-effect sensor and print the results directly to my laptop.

When the magnet is brought close, the output flips. This shows as the green LED turning off and the red LED turning on.

04

Build and mount

The circuit then moved to perfboard as a rapid prototyping solution. A cable sleeve and a JST connector were added for quick attachment and detachment, and a back-up board was made in case any issue came up.

The mechanical team designed the bracket. I gave them the dimensions and asked for the zip-tie points and the cable cover.

05

Getting the ODrive to see it

Initial testing made it look like the ODrive didn't see the sensor. The input never changed on screen, even though the status LEDs worked as expected.

This led to a long debugging process and hours of testing in the ODrive GUI. The issue was simply that the ODrive tool wasn't sampling live, so the sensor's state never updated on screen. This is best shown in Figure 10, where the arrow points to the on/off button for sampling.

Once that was fixed, GPIO 7 was set as the digital input, the minimum endstop was pointed at it, and the "active high" setting was selected. The result is the working slide in the video at the top of this page. I included a brief write-up to document the process for future use.

The ODrive is set up with a revolution limit. When the rover is powered on, the slide moves to the end position, hits the endstop, then runs a set number of revolutions back to the middle position. The ODrive then sets software limits for each end of the linear slide, similar to a 3D printer.

06

Why there is a version 2

The sensor worked, but the magnet was the weak point. The slide was small and the magnet simply didn't hold well. If it happened to fall off, the slide would have no endstop.

The idea of a physical switch was first raised by the software team, and after a brief discussion a version 2 was created. A switch ultimately gives up some precision and speed, but it stays well within what the slide needs. That became the limit switch board.

Speed Boat Electrical Repair

A friend's speed boat whose engine bay filled with water, twice. I repaired the electrical system in 2022 and 2023. I also fixed many mechanical issues with the boat.

01

What I found

I found the boat in a poor state, with a rusty pool of water in the engine bay.

The starter was rusted solid. With it out, I could see the rust had extended to the flywheel teeth behind it.

02

Starter and alternator

After pulling the old starter out and crimping new terminal connections, I replaced the starter. After the boat flooded a second time in 2023, I replaced it once more. That second time, I had to redo all the crimps again.

A new alternator also went in during the 2023 round.

03

Battery and a new fuse

I added an in-line fuse between the battery and the ignition, which has saved an untold number of electronics. This 15 A fuse has blown a number of times, due to poor pre-existing connections and other unknown issues.

I also had to redo the battery cable crimps after they, quite literally, disintegrated when I moved them.

04

Ignition switch

The ignition switch also failed in 2023, which led to me replacing its wires as well. I tagged each one to make sure they went back on in the correct order.

05

Drive hydraulics

The hydraulic pump was also badly corroded, and it fell apart when simply touched. After a long process of finding a replacement, I rewired the connections and got it running again. However, the lines had to be flushed because water had gotten into the hydraulic fluid.

The hydraulic lines themselves were removed and replaced after the seals began to fail. This required us to ratchet strap the drive in an upright position to reach all the threaded connections.

06

Repaired twice

After the first round of repairs it filled with water again, and many of the same parts had to be replaced a second time.

  • 2022. New starter. Redid the crimps, battery cables included. Cleaned the boat.
  • 2023, after the second flood. Another starter, a new alternator, and the crimps again. New hydraulic pump. New ignition switch. Fixed the overhead speakers after a connection broke. Cleaned it again.
  • 2024. New belt, after the old one snapped mid-use.

In 2022 and 2023 there was also a lot of rust to remove. The rust had degraded the health of many components on the boat. Although it was cleaned with a rust solvent and a steel brush, the boat overall remains in a degraded state, as it is simply too expensive to replace everything needed.

These days it starts and runs with little to no issue.

Rover Power and Wiring

During the 2025–2026 design year, I was involved in the overhaul of the power distribution board (PDB) and the new fuse board implementation on the rover.

The rover switched from a 12 V design to a 24 V design. This required a redesigned PDB, which I helped wire by making a number of the XT90, XT60, and XT30 connections.

Two fuse boards were also designed during this time by Aaron Lee, one of the two electrical leads for the 2026–2027 design year.

I was directly involved with both the PDB and the fuse boards during development and later testing.

01

What the board does

These fuse boards were designed for both the ODrive S1 and Pro models. They are housed on the arm of the rover and contain a blade fuse for overcurrent protection, only passing power when the input voltage is within a safe range.

The output stays off when the board is plugged in backwards or an invalid voltage level is read.

Figure 1 shows the older design for the S1. The newer versions, made for both the S1 and the Pro, add the heatsinks shown in Figure 2.

02

Soldering the boards

I soldered both the older and the newer fuse boards. The heatsinks on the newer version were a notable pain to solder on, requiring a large soldering tip, 450 °C of heat, and nearly 5 minutes of strategic heat application.

Smaller components, such as U1, were much easier to deal with. I soldered them both with a hot air gun (Figure 4) and by hand.

Figure 3 shows a number of connections made by me, largely connecting the PDB to each ODrive and NEO 550. Smaller 12 V and 5 V connections remained for software use (Jetson, Pi, etc.) and science use.

All the ODrives are also connected to each other over CAN, which I work with regularly.

03

Testing it

I tested two boards: the older S1 board (no heatsinks) and the newer Pro board (with heatsinks). The numbers here are from the older S1 board. I don't have the Pro data on hand.

The newer S1 board was not tested separately. It is functionally the same circuit, with heatsinks added to carry heat away.

I stepped the input voltage from roughly −32 V up to +40 V and recorded the output at each step.

The output turns on at about 18.4 V and follows the input up to about 34.9 V, where it cuts off. Coming back down, it turns on again at about 32.8 V and drops out at about 17.5 V.

The gap between the on and off points is on purpose. Without it, the board would chatter on and off whenever the voltage sat right at a limit.

With the input reversed, the output never moved more than 0.35 V from zero.

A small note: these boards are supposed to be rated for 30 A. Due to the limits of the available equipment, I could only test up to 10 A, so the full rating is untested. There remains a chance that the board fails internally before the 30 A blade fuse does.

04

Terminals and harnesses

The addition of ring terminals was my recommendation, as it allows us to easily daisy-chain the fuse boards up the arm of the rover.

I also built a number of harnesses on the rover, for the NEO 550 motors, miscellaneous connectors, and the pair of servos that power the hand.

05

A new floor for the electrical box

The bottom of the rover's electrical box was a scratched sheet of acrylic with velcro stuck all over it. In November 2026, another club member and I replaced it.

We designed a new plate in Onshape, laser cut it, and bolted it in. It's larger, cleaner, and there are fewer random holes.

In a moment of frustration, the old one was removed, measured, redesigned, and installed all within 3 hours.

This is only version 1. Some improvements to be made are a laser-engraved Yonder logo, larger screw holes, rounded edges to remove sharp points, and better magnetic mounting for the pegboard attachment seen in Figure 13.

Why a bench

Sorting the motors

We worked together on a majority of the project, in both rain and shine, well into the night. My official task was the electrical system, but I aided in much of the mechanical work.

Where it started

My friend initially got it for free, with a worn deck, torn seats, and a bare console. We had to strip it down to the frame and begin from scratch.

The old flooring was removed and replaced with marine-grade plywood and adhesive. New marine vinyl flooring was added, the seats were replaced, and a new driver's console was installed.

The roof and siding were reused after being cleaned and polished.

02

A plan on paper first

As this was a high school project, I drew a rough schematic of the wiring and got some valuable suggestions from my father.

Each load was estimated, then fused and sized accordingly. Each fuse was rated for its circuit's worst case.

Power leaves the battery through an in-line fuse, then a main cut-off switch, then a lighted switch on the dash, then a 12-circuit fuse panel under the helm. Every load gets its own fuse in that panel and its own dedicated switch.

03

Fuse panel and switches

Each circuit was labeled at its fuse and its switch, as seen in Figure 6. The labels on the panel read stereo, horn, GPS, dome light, DC outlet, and accessory.

The wires end in crimped connectors and are zip-tied into bundles, so any one of them can be followed from fuse to switch.

04

Loads, battery, and wire runs

I wired the Bluetooth radio and its speakers, which sit in the driver's console and the big bench seat. I also wired the fish finder (which doubles as the speedometer), the front lights, the underwater LEDs, and the overhead lights.

The main cut-off switch at the battery kills power to everything. Most of the wiring runs under the deck inside PVC pipe, out of the water and away from feet.

The battery was a leftover we found that still held a charge and was rated for a boat.

05

Charging from the outboard

The outboard has no belt-driven alternator. It charges the battery from a stator instead, a ring of coils under the flywheel that makes power as the flywheel's magnets spin past.

The boat needed a new stator. I fitted it and wired it back into the engine's charging circuit, which feeds the battery.

06

Result

It runs, and continues to this day. In April 2021 it was out on the lake with a new deck, new seats, and new wiring. It has been in use ever since and, besides a summer cleaning, has worked perfectly each year.

Personal project

Buggy Noise Filters

My family's buggy runs a Holley Sniper fuel injection system. The buggy had a notably bad habit of lurching forward at random times, starting rough, and stalling. After a long diagnosis process, the likely culprit was electrical noise on its 12 V wiring reaching the Sniper.

  • Troubleshooting
  • Electrical noise (EMI)
  • Filter design
  • Crimping
System
Holley Sniper fuel injection with an HEI distributor
Source 1
LED light bar
Filter 1
1000 µF electrolytic and two 0.1 µF ceramics, across 12 V and ground at the light bar
Source 2
Ignition
Filter 2
A series resistor with a ceramic capacitor to ground on each side, built in-line
Status
Both installed. Running clean, with no more stalling or lurching.
01

Two sources of noise

The Holley Sniper is a common fuel injection computer among enthusiasts. It reads sensors and fires the fuel injectors. It shares a 12 V wiring connection with everything else on the buggy, so noise on the wiring can easily end up in what the computer reads.

Two things were making noise: the LED light bar and the ignition.

02

Light bar filter

An LED light bar doesn't draw a steady current. The driver for the LEDs switches the current on and off extraordinarily fast, and each switch puts noise onto the 12 V line. A well-designed light has parts to soak up this noise, but the one on the buggy didn't.

The filter I made is three capacitors from the 12 V line to ground. The big 1000 µF capacitor works as a small battery to draw from, supplying the LEDs when a momentary drop occurs.

The two smaller 0.1 µF ceramic capacitors catch any fast noise not caught by the electrolytic capacitor, typically created by the quick switching of the LEDs.

It sits close to the light bar's power connection, so the noise is filtered at its source. To further help with any noise, ferrite cores and EMI shielding tape were also used to cut down on outside EMI.

03

Ignition filter

Every time the distributor fires the coil, it kicks a sharp spike onto the ignition's 12 V line. That line also runs to the fuel injection system.

This filter goes in-line on the positive wire and works in three stages:

  • First capacitor. It sits on the noisy ignition side and sends the sharpest, fastest spikes, like coil ringing, straight to ground before they reach the resistor.
  • Resistor. It sits in series with the positive wire. It holds back fast current spikes and gives the second capacitor something to work against.
  • Second capacitor. It sits on the clean side. With the resistor it forms a low-pass filter that smooths out what's left, and it keeps a small reserve of charge for whatever is connected.

The filter has three leads: red in, red out, and a black ground wire with a ring terminal that bolts to the frame. I didn't write the part values down when I built it, so the drawing shows the layout and not the values.

I built it right on the wire, crimped a terminal on each end, and sealed it in heat shrink.

04

Result

With both filters in, the fuel injection has run clean, removing the stalling and random lurching that once occurred.
