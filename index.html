Sierra College circuits lab · 2025

8-Bit Light Meter

A light meter made from logic chips. No microcontroller, no code. It turns light into an 8-bit number, subtracts the room's light, and shows what's left on LEDs.

It started as a lab in my circuits class at Sierra College. We were handed an outline of the circuit, and the rest was ours to figure out. Afterward I turned mine into my first PCB.

  • Logic design
  • ADC
  • Breadboarding
  • Debugging
  • First PCB
Sensor
Photoresistor in a Wheatstone bridge
Converter
ADC0804, 8-bit (256 levels)
Zeroing
2 × 74HC86 XOR, 2 × 74LS83 adder
Input
8-position DIP switch
Display
One LED per bit
Reset
Push button on the converter chip
Supply
5 V and 12 V
Status
Breadboard working. PCB made, not assembled.
01

Reading the light

A photoresistor sits in one arm of a Wheatstone bridge. As the light changes, the bridge output swings between 0 and 5 V, dark to bright.

The ADC0804 turns that voltage into a number from 0 to 255.

Picking the bridge resistors was trial and error. Change a value, cover the sensor, watch the meter, and repeat until the output sat near 0 V in the dark and near 5 V in the light.

02

Zeroing out the room

A room is never fully dark, so the raw reading never starts at zero. Eight switches along the bottom set a number to subtract.

XOR gates and two 4-bit adders take that number off the reading, so the display reads zero in normal room light. That's two's complement subtraction, which I had to learn in order to build it.

03

Hours of debugging

This was a long lab, and most of the hours went into finding out why something didn't work. The problems that ate the most time:

  • Bridge resistors. Getting a clean 0 V and a clean 5 V out of the bridge.
  • LED resistors. Bright enough to read, dim enough to look at.
  • Reset button. The converter needed a push-button reset. I ended up pulling pins off the button and reworking the wiring so it only touches ground while it's pressed.
  • Zeroing. Built from the switches and XOR gates, once I understood how binary subtraction works.
  • Moving to chips. Scaling up from transistor circuits to logic chips, and debugging each chip as it went in.
  • Layout. Arranging the LEDs and wires so I could trace any signal by eye. That paid off every time something broke.

A fair share of those hours went to debugging classmates' circuits too.

04

From breadboard to PCB

Once the breadboard worked, I laid the circuit out as a PCB. Nobody assigned that part. It was my first board.

The boards came back and the chips fit. I haven't finished soldering it yet.

Yonder Dynamics · 2026

Limit Switch Endstop

A small board that tells the rover's ODrive when the linear slide has hit the end of its travel. It's one switch and one transistor, and it can only say OK while it has power and its wiring is intact.

  • Altium
  • LTspice
  • 2-layer PCB
  • Hand soldered
Part no.
YD-ENDSTOP-001, Rev A
Reads into
GPIO 7 on the ODrive, set as the minimum endstop. An S1 for now. A Pro works too.
Supply
5 V from the ODrive
Output
About 4.9 V at rest. 0 V when pressed, unplugged, or unpowered.
Switch
Lever switch on its normally-closed contact, or a remote switch on J2
Board
2 layers, ground on both
Cost
About $10 a board, in a batch of five
Status
Bench tested. Testing on the slide is next.
01

What it has to do

The rover's arm rides on a linear slide, driven by a NEO motor through an ODrive. The ODrive needs to know where the end of the slide is, so it has a fixed point to measure from.

Version 1 used a hall-effect sensor. This one uses a plain mechanical switch.

The hall sensor needs a magnet on the slide, and on a slide this small the magnet never fit or held well. If it falls off, there is no end stop. The software team also liked the idea of a physical switch.

A switch is slower and less exact than a hall sensor. For this slide that doesn't matter. It is well inside the tolerance.

02

The circuit

The switch is wired through its normally-closed contact. At rest the loop is closed, which turns on a PNP transistor, and the transistor puts about 5 V on the ODrive's input. The LED is on. That is the board saying OK.

Press the lever and the loop opens. The transistor turns off, a 10 kΩ resistor pulls the signal down to 0 V, and the LED goes out. The ODrive reads that as the end of the slide.

A cut cable, an unplugged connector, or a dead 5 V supply all look the same as a pressed lever. The board has to be powered and wired correctly to say OK, so the likely failures read as stop.

That is also why the transistor is a PNP on the high side. The ODrive's input has no pull resistor of its own, and I wanted the circuit to spend energy saying OK, not saying stop.

Small capacitors filter the switch and the signal line, and the ODrive adds 50 ms of its own debounce.

03

The layout

Two layers, ground poured on both. Everything sits on the top side, because I solder these by hand.

Three test points (switch, signal, ground), each big enough to clip a probe onto.

04

Build and bench test

Boards from JLCPCB, parts from Digi-Key, soldered by me.

On the bench it powers up, and the signal and the LED follow the lever.

05

What's next

Testing on the slide is waiting on a mount from the mechanical team and the ODrive setup. When that's done, a video of the slide finding its end goes here.

The ODrive side is short: GPIO 7 as a digital input, set as the minimum endstop, active-high off, 50 ms debounce, and a slow homing speed to start.

Yonder Dynamics · 2026 · Version 1

Hall-Effect Endstop

Version 1 of the rover's end-of-travel sensor. It went from a notebook sketch to a breadboard to a hand-wired board on the slide.

  • Hall-effect sensor
  • Breadboarding
  • Perfboard
  • ODrive
Sensor
A3144 hall-effect sensor
Output
N-channel transistor, 10 kΩ, 0.1 µF
Reads into
ODrive, pin 7, set as the minimum endstop
Slide
15.75 in of travel
Size limit
0.75 in tall
Build
Hand-wired perfboard with a status LED
Status
Replaced by version 2
01

The job

The rover's arm rides on a linear slide, which gives the arm one more direction to move. Before the ODrive can position the arm, it has to find one end of the rail and call that zero.

That takes a sensor at the end of the rail and a homing routine in the ODrive.

02

On paper first

I worked it out in my notebook first: which ODrive pins to use, where the sensor sits on the rail, the circuit, and the homing steps.

One note on that page carried straight into version 2. A limit switch should be wired normally closed, so a broken wire stops the slide instead of letting it run.

03

Breadboard test

The first test was on a breadboard, with an ESP32 reading the sensor and printing the result to my laptop.

Bring a magnet close and the output flips. The LEDs show it.

04

Build and mount

Then the circuit moved to perfboard, with a status LED and a sleeved cable running to the ODrive.

The mechanical team designed the bracket. I gave them the dimensions and asked for the zip-tie points and the cable cover.

05

Getting the ODrive to see it

At first the ODrive didn't see the sensor at all. The input wasn't being detected, and nothing changed in the ODrive tool no matter what the sensor did.

That turned into a long goose chase. The cause was one setting: sampling was turned off on that input. With pin 7 set as a digital input, the minimum endstop pointed at it and switched on, and "active high" set to match the sensor, it worked.

I wrote the settings up for the team, along with how to watch the sensor's state live in the ODrive tool. After that, the sensor stopped the slide at the end of its travel.

06

Why there is a version 2

The sensor worked. The magnet was the weak point. The slide is small, and the magnet wouldn't fit or hold well on it. If it fell off, the slide would have no end stop at all.

The software team also liked the idea of a physical switch. A switch gives up some speed and precision, but it stays well within what the slide needs. That became the limit switch board.

Rover Power and Wiring

Every motor controller on the rover's arm sits behind a small fuse board. I soldered those boards, tested one across its whole voltage range, and I build the rover's wire harnesses.

The fuse boards were designed by Aaron Lee, one of the team's two electrical leads.

  • Soldering
  • Testing
  • Crimping
  • Harnesses
Board
ODrive S1 fuse board, Mark 2
Design
Aaron Lee
My part
Soldering, testing, and harnesses. I also proposed using these boards on the arm, and ring terminals for the power connections.
Turns on
About 18.4 V
Cuts off
About 34.9 V
Reversed
Output stays within 0.35 V of zero
Later
Became the base for the ODrive Pro version
01

What the board does

Each board sits between the battery and one ODrive. It holds a blade fuse and only passes power when the input voltage is in a safe range.

Too low, too high, or plugged in backwards, and the output stays off.

Putting these boards on the arm was my recommendation. Most of them live there now.

02

Soldering the boards

I soldered the fuse boards. They're surface-mount: two big power transistors, a small control chip, and the resistors around it.

In the rover's electrical box, where part of the wiring is mine, a fuse board sits between two ODrives.

03

Testing it

I stepped the input from about −32 V up to +40 V and wrote down the output at each step.

The output turns on at about 18.4 V and follows the input up to about 34.9 V, where it cuts off. Coming back down, it turns on again at about 32.8 V and drops out at about 17.5 V.

The gap between the on and off points is on purpose. Without it, the board would chatter on and off whenever the voltage sat right at a limit.

With the input reversed, the output never moved more than 0.35 V from zero.

One test is still open. My equipment tops out at 10 A, which isn't enough to find out whether the fuse blows before the board gives out.

04

Terminals and harnesses

I pushed for ring terminals on the power connections. They bolt down, and they let power pass from one board to the next in a chain.

I also build the rover's harnesses: motor leads, servo power for the hand, and connectors.

05

A new floor for the electrical box

The bottom of the rover's electrical box was a scratched sheet of acrylic with velcro stuck all over it. In November 2026, another club member and I replaced it.

We designed a new plate in Onshape, laser cut it, and bolted it in. It's larger, it's cleaner, and the screw holes are where they should be. I also helped make the mounting system.

Design to installed took about three hours.

This is version 1. Still to come: bigger screw holes so it goes on faster, rounded corners so nothing is sharp, and the Yonder logo engraved in it.

Personal project · 2020–21

Pontoon Boat Rebuild

A worn-out pontoon boat, stripped to the frame and rebuilt with a friend and his grandfather, who let me join the project. They ran the mechanical side, including the engine rebuild. The electrical system was mine, engine wiring included, and all three of us worked on everything else.

Mercury 40 hp FourStroke outboard

01

Where it started

It showed up with a worn deck, torn seats, and a bare console. We took it down to the frame.

The old floor came out. New plywood went down on marine adhesive, then marine flooring, seats, and a new driver's console. There was a lot of metal polishing.

02

A plan on paper first

Before I ran a single wire, I drew the whole system: every load, the fuse for each one, and the wire size for the current it carries.

Power leaves the battery through an in-line fuse, then a main cut-off switch, then a lighted switch on the dash, then a 12-circuit fuse panel under the helm. Every load gets its own fuse and its own switch.

03

Fuse panel and switches

Each circuit lands on its own labeled fuse: stereo, horn, GPS, dome light, DC outlet, accessory.

The wires end in crimped connectors and are zip-tied into bundles, so any one of them can be followed from fuse to switch.

04

Loads, battery, and wire runs

I wired the Bluetooth radio and its speakers, which sit in the driver's console and the big bench seat. I also wired the fish finder (which doubles as the speedometer), the front lights, the underwater LEDs, and the overhead lights.

A kill switch at the battery cuts power to everything. Most of the wiring runs under the deck inside PVC pipe, out of the water and away from feet.

The battery was a leftover we found that still held a charge and was rated for a boat.

05

Charging from the outboard

The outboard has no belt-driven alternator. It charges the battery from a stator, a ring of coils under the flywheel that makes power as the flywheel's magnets spin past.

The boat needed a new stator. I fitted it and wired its output to the battery. The regulator and rectifier were fine.

The cable connections were not, so I redid the crimps.

06

Result

It runs. In April 2021 it was out on the lake with a new deck, new seats, and new wiring, and it has been in use for about six years. I'm still the one who gets called when something on it breaks.

Personal project

Buggy Noise Filters

My family's buggy runs a Holley Sniper fuel injection system. Two things on the buggy were putting electrical noise on its 12 V wiring, and that noise was reaching the Sniper. I found both sources and built a filter for each.

  • Troubleshooting
  • Electrical noise (EMI)
  • Filter design
  • Crimping
System
Holley Sniper fuel injection with an HEI distributor
Source 1
LED light bar
Filter 1
1000 µF electrolytic and two 0.1 µF ceramics, across 12 V and ground at the light bar
Source 2
Ignition
Filter 2
A series resistor with a ceramic capacitor to ground on each side, built in-line
Status
Both installed. Running clean.
01

Two sources of noise

The Sniper is a small computer that reads sensors and fires the injectors. It shares its 12 V wiring with everything else on the buggy, so noise on that wiring can end up in what the computer reads.

Two things were making noise. One was the LED light bar. The other was the ignition.

02

Light bar filter

An LED light bar doesn't draw steady current. Its driver switches the current on and off very fast, and each switch tugs on the 12 V line. A well-designed light has parts inside to soak that up. This one didn't have enough.

My filter is three capacitors across 12 V and ground. The big 1000 µF one works like a small tank next to the light. It feeds the light's quick gulps of current, so they aren't pulled through the buggy's wiring.

The two small 0.1 µF ceramics catch the fastest noise, which the big capacitor is too slow to handle.

It sits as close to the light bar's power wires as I could get it. That way the noise is shorted out at the source, before the wiring can carry it anywhere.

03

Ignition filter

Every time the distributor fires the coil, it kicks a sharp spike onto the ignition's 12 V line. That line also runs to the fuel injection system.

This filter goes in-line on the positive wire and works in three stages:

  • First capacitor. It sits on the noisy ignition side and sends the sharpest, fastest spikes, like coil ringing and alternator whine, straight to ground before they reach the resistor.
  • Resistor. It sits in series with the positive wire. It holds back fast current spikes and gives the second capacitor something to work against.
  • Second capacitor. It sits on the clean side. With the resistor it forms a low-pass filter that smooths out what's left, and it keeps a small reserve of charge for whatever is connected.

The filter has three leads: red in, red out, and a black ground wire with a ring terminal that bolts to the frame. I didn't write the part values down when I built it, so the drawing shows the layout and not the values.

I built it right on the wire, crimped a terminal on each end, and sealed it in heat shrink.

04

Result

With both filters in, the fuel injection has run clean.
